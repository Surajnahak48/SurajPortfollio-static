Choosing a baseline before you train anything

October 12, 2026    Suraj Nahak

BaselinesEvaluationML

I used to start projects the fun way: open a notebook, load the data, and go straight for the interesting model. A week later I'd have an F1 of 0.81 and no idea whether that was good. Was the problem easy? Was 0.81 what you'd get by guessing? I couldn't say, because I'd never checked.

These days the first thing I build is the most boring model I can think of. It takes ten minutes and it changes how I read every result after it.

Start embarrassingly simple

For classification, my first baseline is "always predict the most common class". For regression, it's "always predict the mean". It sounds pointless, but it tells you the floor. If 92% of your labels are the same class, 92% accuracy is not an achievement. It's the starting line.

from sklearn.dummy import DummyClassifier

baseline = DummyClassifier(strategy="most_frequent")
baseline.fit(X_train, y_train)
print("floor:", baseline.score(X_test, y_test))

After that I add one step up: a logistic regression or a small gradient-boosted tree with default settings. No tuning, no feature engineering beyond what's needed to make it run. This is the number a fancier model has to clearly beat to be worth the extra complexity.

Decide the metric before you look at results

This one took me a while to learn. If you pick the metric after seeing the numbers, you'll pick whichever one makes your model look best, without even meaning to. So I write it down first, usually in the README: "We care about recall on the positive class at 90% precision." Once it's written down, I'm much less likely to quietly move the goalposts later.

Keep the baseline in every table

Every results table I share has the baseline as its first row. It's a small habit, but it stops conversations like "the new model gets 0.84!" from going anywhere until someone asks "compared to what?"

It also catches bugs. More than once, a model that looked great turned out to barely beat the dummy, and that's what made me go looking for the leak in the pipeline.

When the baseline wins

Sometimes the simple model is good enough. That's not a failure. A logistic regression that's 2% worse but trains in seconds, is easy to explain, and never surprises anyone in production is often the right thing to ship. Having the baseline there is what lets you make that call on purpose instead of by accident.

So: dumb model first, metric written down, baseline in every table. It's not glamorous, but it's saved me more time than any clever trick.
